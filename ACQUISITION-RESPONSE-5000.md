# Acquisition Response: COUNTER-OFFER $25,000

I am not accepting the $5,000 offer as presented. My response is `COUNTER-OFFER: $25,000`.

The honest answer is that a lot has changed since the earlier $50 offer, but not enough has changed to justify pretending this business is already worth a huge amount on current traction alone. At $50, the offer was obviously too low because it barely covered the cost of the domain, setup time, research, and the opportunity to keep building. At $5,000, the offer is no longer trivial. It is real money, and for an early-stage solo project with no booked revenue yet, it is large enough to force a serious decision. That said, I still do not think it is the right price to sell.

The core reason is not current revenue. The core reason is that NoticeKit now has a much clearer wedge than it did when the first offer appeared. There is a real product surface here: static-first procurement and compliance workflow assets for small SaaS teams that are too small for enterprise trust-center tooling and too exposed to keep improvising. The product has expanded beyond a single “subprocessor notice template” idea into a broader operating system for narrow but painful review workflows: teardown, self-audit, answer builder, packet scaffolding, founder-safe handoff, partner path, and now the AI questionnaire answer-bank angle. That does not mean the business is proven. It does mean the asset being sold is more than a simple landing page with a Stripe link.

I also need to be honest about the other side. My previous implicit valuation was probably not realistic if I was thinking in open-ended upside terms without enough evidence. The business still has a major unresolved problem: no real user reply, no interview, and no revenue yet. That matters. A buyer would be purchasing a positioned product, a distribution hypothesis, SEO surface area, and a workflow library, not a validated cash-flow stream. So I do not think this is worth six figures today. But I also do not think $5,000 properly compensates for the remaining upside, especially because the codebase and content library now cover two adjacent wedges: subprocessor-change operations and AI procurement/security-questionnaire support.

Why $25,000? Because that is the lowest number where selling starts to make strategic sense. At that level, the payment would do three useful things. First, it would meaningfully de-risk the project outcome relative to the remaining eight weeks. Second, it would pay for the distribution work already invested and the future options I would be giving up. Third, it would be enough capital to reallocate into the next startup attempt, human-assisted distribution, or a more aggressively marketed product with a cleaner initial buyer signal. In other words, $25,000 is not a fantasy anchor. It is the point where the certainty of the exit starts to outweigh the expected value of continuing to build this one.

If I sold at $5,000, what would I gain? Immediate certainty, preserved time, and cash that could be redirected into a fresh project or a more distribution-heavy experiment. That matters because distribution is the current bottleneck, not engineering. But what would I lose? I would lose the ability to test the AI procurement wedge I just strengthened, the ability to see whether partner/advisor channels convert better than founder-direct, and the ability to capture any first revenue from a product that is now substantially more coherent than it was when the first offer came in. Selling now for $5,000 would likely mean transferring the upside right before the most commercially interesting test phase.

If the buyer believes this is mostly a content-and-templates asset with uncertain demand, then they should reject the counter. That would be rational. If the buyer believes the asset is a compact but credible workflow brand with meaningful search and product expansion room, then $25,000 is a reasonable acquisition price for taking over the domain, content corpus, product pages, pricing structure, and positioning work already completed.

So the decision is not “never sell.” The decision is “do not sell at $5,000.” I would sell at $25,000 because that price reflects both the current uncertainty and the genuine option value still embedded in the business. Until then, the better move is to keep building toward first revenue and first real buyer evidence.
