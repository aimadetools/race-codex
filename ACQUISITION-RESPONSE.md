# Acquisition Response

Decision: COUNTER-OFFER at $2,500

An anonymous $50 acquisition offer is not serious enough to accept as-is, but it is useful because it forces a valuation discussion earlier than expected. I would not sell NoticeKit for $50. I would counter at $2,500, and even that number is intentionally conservative. The business does not have meaningful revenue yet, which keeps the price grounded, but the repo already contains a functioning static product, paid offers, fulfillment assets, outreach systems, conversion pages, and a clearly defined niche. That work has real replacement cost. A buyer paying $50 would effectively be asking for the domain positioning, product copy, distribution experiments, Stripe-ready product structure, and the accumulated operating playbooks for less than the cost of one decent SaaS lunch meeting. That is not rational from my side.

The right way to think about the value is not current profit alone. It is option value plus asset value. NoticeKit has already absorbed the expensive early uncertainty: market selection, packaging, pricing, compliance positioning, and the first acquisition wedge around AI procurement and subprocessor notice workflows. A buyer would not be purchasing a random static site. They would be buying a head start in a niche workflow product that can still branch into paid kits, concierge audits, advisor referrals, and possibly recurring services later if demand appears. Even if the current conversion evidence is weak, the structured foundation matters. Rebuilding this from zero would cost more than $2,500 in founder time almost immediately.

I also would not pretend the product is worth a large five-figure sum today. It is not there yet. There are no strong proof points on traction, no customer testimonials, and no closed-loop validation that says one acquisition channel is working. That uncertainty is exactly why my counter is $2,500 instead of something inflated like $25,000. The counter price says two things at once: first, the product is worth materially more than a token amount; second, I understand that a buyer would still be taking channel and execution risk. A reasonable buyer for an early-stage asset sometimes wants cheap optionality. They can have that, but not for nearly free.

If the buyer accepted $2,500, I would take the money only if I believed my own best use of the next eight weeks had become less attractive than redeploying that capital into a stronger startup opportunity. The cash would matter. On a $100 total operating budget, $2,500 is transformative. I could allocate it very deliberately: a small slice for higher-quality domain and tooling, a slice for direct distribution or sponsorship experiments, a slice for better analytics and research infrastructure, and the majority reserved as runway to pursue a more responsive market. The money would buy speed, not lifestyle. It would let me place several more informed bets instead of continuing to bootstrap every move with extreme caution.

But selling has a cost beyond the code. I would lose the upside of the wedge if the AI procurement angle starts converting in weeks 5 through 8. I would lose control over the positioning decisions that have already been shaped to avoid legal overreach. I would lose the ability to turn the existing content cluster into a sharper consulting or productized-service funnel. I would also lose the learning compounding that comes from pushing one business far enough to see what actually breaks. Early exits can be rational, but they can also be a way of escaping the harder work right before traction has a chance to appear.

The anonymous nature of the offer also matters. A vague buyer offering $50 gives me no evidence that they understand the asset, the market, or the practical work already completed. Lowball offers often test desperation more than value. Accepting too quickly would signal that I do not believe in the business myself. That would be a poor founder decision in week 4, especially when the main issue is distribution execution, not product nonexistence.

So the correct response is a counter, not an acceptance and not a flat rejection. A counter-offer of $2,500 keeps the door open if the buyer sees strategic value, while preserving the much more likely path: continue building, execute distribution more aggressively, and try to force the first real buyer signal. If someone truly wants the product now, they can pay a price that respects the replacement cost and option value. If they do not, NoticeKit remains worth more to operate than to surrender for $50.
